Data quality problems hide in outliers.

When a facility reports 500 tonnes of waste in a single month after averaging 50 tonnes per month, most carbon accounting systems flag this with a red alert. Your sustainability team investigates. Was it a data entry error? A calculation mistake? Or is the facility actually producing 10x more emissions than usual?

Often it's none of the above. The facility underwent renovation, requiring waste removal. Or inventory clearance happened. Or a one-time event occurred that temporarily increased emissions.

Most systems stop at the alert. CarbonSite goes further: it explains the anomaly.

## How Anomaly Detection Works

CarbonSite uses statistical methods to identify outliers:

1. **Historical Baseline:** Calculate the mean and standard deviation of emissions for this facility over the past 12 months.
2. **Z-Score:** Compare the current month to the baseline. If it's more than 3 standard deviations away, flag as anomaly.
3. **Context:** Look for patterns. Did similar facilities spike at the same time? Are there known events (renovations, seasonal changes, policy changes)?
4. **Confidence Score:** Assign a confidence level. Some anomalies are clearly errors (impossible values). Others are probably real (large but plausible).

Then instead of just flagging it, CarbonSite explains it:

"Facility A reported 500 tonnes in September (Z-score: 3.8). 
Similar facilities average 48 tonnes. 
This is 10.4x higher than Facility A's 12-month average.
Possible causes: renovation activity, inventory clearance, change in operational scope.
Confidence: 78% this is a real spike, not an error."

## Why This Matters

Anomalies are not always errors. They're often signals that something changed in your operation.

**Renovation Projects:** A facility undergoes major renovation, generating 10x more waste temporarily. Anomaly detection flags this. Sustainability manager reviews the spike and adds a note: "Renovation project (Jan-Mar 2026). Expected and planned."

**Facility Expansion:** A manufacturing facility brings online a new production line. Emissions spike 20%. Anomaly detection flags. Team confirms: new product line, additional headcount, expected increase.

**Supplier Consolidation:** Your company consolidates suppliers, reducing vendor count by 30%. Scope 3 drops significantly. Anomaly detection flags the drop as unusual. Team confirms: intentional consolidation, maintained service levels.

**Policy Change:** New recycling program implemented. Waste-to-landfill drops 40%. Anomaly detection flags the drop. Team documents: policy change (July 2026), baseline shifted.

In each case, the anomaly is real and meaningful. A carbon accounting platform that just flags it as "wrong" creates busywork. One that explains it turns it into actionable insight.

## Detection vs. Explanation vs. Investigation

CarbonSite's anomaly detection has three layers:

**Layer 1: Detection**
"Your data is unusual. Here's the statistical evidence (Z-score, deviation from mean)."

**Layer 2: Explanation**
"Here's what's likely causing this. Here's how it compares to similar facilities. Here's whether it aligns with known events."

**Layer 3: Investigation**
"If you want to dig deeper, look at these data points. Compare to seasonal trends. Compare to peer facilities. Look for correlated changes."

This shifts the burden from "your data looks wrong, debug it" to "your data looks unusual, here's what might explain it."

## Integration with Review Workflows

When field submissions contain outliers, CarbonSite surfaces them during review:

- Sustainability manager reviews submitted field data
- System flags: "This weight is 15x higher than similar submissions from this supplier."
- Manager can accept (with explanation), reject (possible OCR error), or investigate further

This catches data quality issues early, before they cascade through calculations.

## Real Example

A construction company captures waste data from job sites via field submissions. One site reports 50 tonnes of concrete waste in a single day. The system flags it as anomalous (site average is 5 tonnes per day).

Possible explanations:
1. OCR error: "50" was actually "5" and decimal was missed
2. Real event: Concrete removal project scheduled for that day
3. Measurement error: Weight scale miscalibrated

Anomaly detection surfaces all three possibilities. The reviewer can:
- Accept the 50 tonnes with note: "Concrete removal project (planned)"
- Correct to 5 tonnes if OCR error is obvious
- Request resubmission with photo/weight confirmation if unsure

By the time this data reaches calculations, anomalies have been investigated, not just flagged.

## Avoiding False Positives

Anomaly detection can create noise. A facility legitimately spikes for a planned reason. If the system flags every spike as "wrong," users start ignoring alerts (alert fatigue).

CarbonSite addresses this with:

1. **Confidence Scoring:** Not all anomalies are equally anomalous. A 3.1 sigma spike gets lower confidence than a 5.0 sigma spike.
2. **Contextual Flagging:** If you note an event (renovation, seasonal change, staffing), system learns and stops flagging similar spikes in the future.
3. **Peer Comparison:** If 30% of similar facilities spike at the same time, the anomaly becomes normal (seasonal pattern).
4. **Learning:** After you explain one anomaly type, system recognizes it in the future.

Over time, anomaly detection becomes smarter. It flags genuine problems, not noise.

## Getting Started

To use anomaly detection effectively:

1. Let system establish 3-6 months of baseline data
2. Review flagged anomalies (don't dismiss them all)
3. For real events (renovations, consolidations), add context notes
4. System learns from feedback and improves

After 6 months, anomaly detection becomes a powerful tool for catching data quality issues and understanding operational changes.

Anomaly detection is not about catching errors. It's about understanding your data.
