# Anomaly Detection in Carbon Data: Catching the Outliers

A sustainability manager at a manufacturing facility submitted an activity record: 1,200 tonnes of waste to landfill on a single day.

No one flagged it.

Three months later, the auditor caught it. The facility's average daily waste was 50 tonnes. One day of 1,200 tonnes? Statistical impossibility. Either a data entry error (12 tonnes mistyped as 1,200) or fraud (inflating waste to claim larger carbon offset budget).

By then, the number had been baked into quarterly reports, executive dashboards, and investor disclosures.

The report had to be reissued. The auditor noted "Control deficiency." The CFO was unhappy.

This happens constantly in carbon accounting. A single outlier—a typo, a unit conversion mistake, a misplaced decimal—corrupts the entire dataset.

Most platforms let these slip through.

## The Outlier Problem

Why do outliers persist?

1. **Manual review is slow.** A sustainability manager reviewing 50 activity records per day can't spot every outlier.
2. **No statistical baseline.** What's "normal"? Without historical context, it's hard to say. (Is 100 kg of CO₂e per tonne of material normal? Or 500 kg?)
3. **Humans trust round numbers.** A facility reports 1,000 tonnes of waste. Sounds plausible, round number. No one questions it.
4. **Statistical thinking is rare.** Most sustainability professionals aren't statisticians. Z-scores? Standard deviation? Not familiar.

Result: ~5–10% of carbon accounting data contains outliers. Your reports are confidently reporting the wrong number.

## The Regulatory Signal

CSRD requires internal controls over emissions reporting. Catching outliers is a key control.

SEC climate rule (proposed) requires "reasonable assurance" over emissions calculations. Manual spot-checking doesn't qualify.

Auditors increasingly ask: "What controls do you have in place to catch bad data?"

If your answer is "We review it manually," auditors note it as a deficiency.

If your answer is "We use statistical anomaly detection, plus manual review of flagged records," auditors approve it.

## How Anomaly Detection Works

CarbonSite uses a multi-layered approach:

### Layer 1: Z-Score Anomalies

For each emission category and facility, we calculate:
- **Mean** (average weight of waste submitted)
- **Standard deviation** (how much it typically varies)

If a record falls >3 standard deviations from the mean, it's flagged. (3σ = 0.27% of normal data; anything beyond is statistically unusual.)

Example:
- Facility submits 50 kg of waste per day, on average.
- Standard deviation: 10 kg (some days 40 kg, some days 60 kg).
- A submission of 100 kg is ~5 standard deviations out. Flagged.

### Layer 2: Domain-Specific Rules

Beyond statistics, we apply business rules:

- **Weight thresholds** (facility capacity). A waste bin can't hold 10,000 tonnes of waste.
- **Emission factor bounds**. CO₂e per tonne should be between 0.1 and 10,000. Anything outside is suspicious.
- **Unit conversions**. If someone entered "1,200" in a weight field but selected "litres," that's likely a unit mistake.
- **Facility capacity checks**. If a facility's max vehicle capacity is 20 tonnes per trip, a single trip of 50 tonnes is impossible.

### Layer 3: Temporal Patterns

Are there day-of-week patterns? (Manufacturing plants often have lower emissions on weekends.)

Are there seasonal patterns? (Heating fuel consumption peaks in winter.)

Does this record fit the seasonal pattern? If a facility usually consumes 5,000 kWh of electricity in July but this July shows 50,000 kWh, that's flagged.

## What Gets Flagged (And Why)

When a record is flagged, the system explains it in human terms:

**Example 1: Unit confusion**
"This record shows 1,200 litres of fuel, but your facility's tank capacity is 500 litres. Possible data entry error (was it 120 litres?). Please review."

**Example 2: Facility capacity**
"This waste facility processed 5,000 tonnes in one day. Your facility's average is 200 tonnes/day. This is 25x normal. Possible OCR error or double-counting? Please verify with site manager."

**Example 3: Seasonal anomaly**
"January electricity consumption is 10,000 kWh. Your winter average is 12,000 kWh. This is unusually low (within normal range). No flag, but noteworthy if the facility was offline."

## The Review Workflow

Not all flags result in rejections.

1. **Record submitted.** The system runs anomaly detection.
2. **Flagged records go to review queue.** Sustainability manager sees: "Waste submission: 1,200 tonnes. Status: ⚠ Anomaly detected. 25x above average."
3. **Manager investigates.** (Was there a special event? A one-time bulk cleanup? A data entry error?)
4. **Manager approves or rejects.** If legitimate, click "Override" + comment why. If an error, reject + ask for correction.
5. **Record locked.** Once approved, can't be modified (audit trail).

The key: **anomaly detection reduces the review burden by 80%**, because reviewers focus on the 1–2% of records that are actually suspicious, instead of manually reviewing everything.

## Common False Positives (And How We Handle Them)

Anomaly detection isn't perfect. Sometimes legitimate data gets flagged.

Example: A facility runs a one-time bulk cleanup and submits 10x normal waste. Legitimate. But it triggers the anomaly flag.

CarbonSite's solution: **provide context.**

The review queue shows: "Anomaly detected: 10x above average. Facility submitted note: 'One-time cleanup after equipment replacement.'"

The reviewer can see the contextual note and approve with one click.

Over time, the system learns which types of "anomalies" are false positives (one-time events, seasonal spikes) and refines the thresholds.
