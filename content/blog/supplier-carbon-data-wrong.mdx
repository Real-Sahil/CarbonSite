Your supplier's carbon footprint is wrong. Not because they're dishonest. Because they estimated it.

A small logistics company with 50 delivery trucks doesn't have a sophisticated carbon accounting system. When you ask for their emissions, they pull up a third-party LCA study from 2023 and say: "Our industry averages about 200 kg CO2e per delivery. We do about 10,000 deliveries per year, so our annual emissions are roughly 2,000 tonnes."

This is a guess. It's not based on their actual fuel consumption, vehicle fleet composition, or routing data. It's based on an industry benchmark.

When you use this in your Scope 3 calculations, you're incorporating someone else's estimate into your report. An auditor will notice.

## Why Supplier Data Is Unreliable

**Reason 1: Suppliers Don't Measure**
Most small-to-mid-market suppliers don't have emissions tracking systems. They estimate based on industry averages or simple rules of thumb.

**Reason 2: Data Is Outdated**
LCA studies are conducted every few years. A supplier's 2023 emissions study doesn't reflect 2026 operational changes (new fleet, route optimization, staffing changes).

**Reason 3: Scope Mismatch**
You ask for Scope 1 + 2 emissions. Supplier provides LCA data that includes Scope 3 (their supply chain). You end up double-counting.

**Reason 4: Incentives Are Misaligned**
If a supplier can report lower emissions, they might. If they expect emissions to be used against them in supplier scoring, they might inflate them. Either way, data is distorted.

**Reason 5: Definition Ambiguity**
Your definition of "emissions per delivery" might differ from their definition of "emissions per vehicle per day." You're comparing different things.

## The CarbonSite Approach: Primary Evidence

Instead of asking suppliers for estimates, CarbonSite captures primary evidence:

1. **Capture:** Field worker photographs a delivery ticket showing: vehicle, route, weight, fuel type
2. **Extract:** OCR pulls structured data (vendor, distance, vehicle type, payload)
3. **Calculate:** Calculation engine applies factors for vehicle type and distance
4. **Accumulate:** Over 3-6 months, build transaction-level history from this supplier
5. **Learn:** Use historical data to identify patterns and fill gaps

After 6 months of capturing delivery tickets:

| Metric | Before | After |
|--------|--------|-------|
| Data basis | Industry LCA (2023) | Primary transaction data (2026) |
| Confidence | ~40% (it's an estimate) | ~85% (real transactions) |
| Seasonality visible | No | Yes (Q4 higher volumes) |
| Route efficiency visible | No | Yes (distance trending down) |
| Vehicle fleet visible | No | Yes (mix of truck types) |

Your Scope 3 now rests on real data, not estimates.

## ML Estimation for Missing Data

You won't capture 100% of transactions. Some suppliers are unresponsive. Some historical data is missing.

CarbonSite fills these gaps with machine learning:

**Pattern Detection:**
- "This supplier's emissions increase 18% in Q4 (holiday season)"
- "Route distance decreased 8% this month (new consolidation center)"
- "Vehicle mix shifted 20% toward smaller trucks (fleet refresh)"

**Gap Filling:**
- Missing 30 deliveries in March? System predicts: "Based on Feb/Apr data, estimated March emissions: 450 tonnes with 78% confidence"

**Confidence Scoring:**
- Captured data: 95% confidence
- ML estimated data: 70-85% confidence (depending on pattern strength)
- Industry average: 40% confidence

Your report can now distinguish: "We captured 80% of transactions directly. For remaining 20%, we applied ML estimation with 75% average confidence."

This is far more defensible than: "We used industry average and hope it's close."

## Real Example: Logistics Supplier

Acme Logistics operates 50 delivery trucks serving your company.

**Month 1-2:** You capture delivery tickets showing:
- 500 deliveries
- Mix: 30 large trucks, 20 medium trucks
- Average distance: 45 km per delivery
- Calculated emissions: 250 tonnes CO2e

**Month 3-6:** Continue capturing tickets:
- Consistent pattern: ~500 deliveries per month
- Seasonal variation: +15% in Q4
- Route optimization: distance trending down 2% per month
- New vehicles: mix shifted 10% toward fuel-efficient trucks

**Month 7-12:** Capture plus ML estimation:
- Estimated Q4 emissions: 280 tonnes (based on seasonal pattern + current baseline)
- Estimated impact of new trucks: saves ~5% fuel per km

Your annual Scope 3 for Acme:
- Months 1-6 (captured): 1,500 tonnes CO2e ± 5% (high confidence)
- Months 7-12 (estimated): 1,650 tonnes CO2e ± 12% (good confidence)
- Total: 3,150 tonnes CO2e ± 8% (combined confidence)

You can report this with confidence. An auditor can see: "80% based on transaction data, 20% based on ML estimation. Both methods shown."

## Anomaly Detection Catches Cheating

What if a supplier intentionally underreports? Or accidentally uses wrong data?

Anomaly detection catches it:

- "Acme reported 50 tonnes for December, but 30-year baseline is 280 tonnes"
- "This is 5.6x lower than expected"
- "Possible causes: fleet breakdown, voluntary reduced operations, data error"

You follow up with the supplier. Either they confirm something changed (makes sense) or you discover a data quality problem (catch it early).

## Getting Started with Supplier Data Quality

1. **Identify top 10-20 suppliers by spend**
2. **Start capturing transactions** via field submissions (photos of delivery tickets, invoices)
3. **Let pattern emerge** over 3-6 months
4. **Use ML estimation** to fill gaps (only for minor suppliers)
5. **Audit the data** annually (check patterns for consistency)

By month 3, you'll see that supplier data from transactions is more reliable than supplier self-reports. By month 6, you'll have genuine Scope 3 data.

## The Big Picture

Supplier carbon data is not inherently wrong. But supplier estimates are. When you switch from estimates to primary evidence — photographs, delivery tickets, transactions — your Scope 3 data becomes defensible.

This is the future of supply chain carbon accounting: not better surveys, but better operations. Field workers with cameras, OCR extracting data, ML filling gaps, and anomaly detection catching errors.

Your suppliers don't need to become carbon accountants. You just need to capture their operational data and let math do the rest.
